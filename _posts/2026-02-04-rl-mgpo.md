I added MGPO natively to [Hugging Face TRL](https://github.com/huggingface/trl/pull/5126) (`#5126`). If `mgpo_reward_base` is set, GRPO advantages get multiplied by a per-prompt weight from the group's success rate. The weight peaks when that rate is near `mgpo_target_success_rate` (default 0.5). Default GRPO is unchanged.

# Introduction

There has been an explosion of research and products around RL for language models, especially GRPO after the [DeepSeek-R1](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2501.12948) paper. Since then there have been hundreds of high quality open and closed source releases. Communities can now improve and build on top of these models instead of starting from scratch. The cost of fine-tuning, whether SFT or RL, has gone down in no small part because of quantization, LoRA, and a pile of GRPO-family tweaks that try to make the learning signal less noisy.

This is a personal project in which I tried one of those variants, MaxEnt-Guided Policy Optimization (MGPO), on a hard visual spatial dataset and compared it to regular GRPO. I wanted to see whether the extra weighting actually changes how the model moves, not just whether a paper reports a higher math score. The dataset is [MulSeT](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2509.02359): two views of the same indoor scene, and the model has to reason about objects relative to each other. The MGPO paper is mainly about math and code. Spatial scenes are a different kind of problem, which is why I wanted to try it here.

# Context: GRPO and the variants around it

[GRPO](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2501.12948) is the algorithm DeepSeek used to train R1-Zero. The original writeup is in [DeepSeekMath](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2402.03300). The idea is simple enough. For one prompt you sample a group of answers, score each one, and treat the group mean and standard deviation as the baseline. You do not need a critic network the way PPO does. Advantage is just how much better or worse this rollout is compared to its siblings.

That group trick is also the source of most of the later papers. If every rollout in the group is correct, or every rollout is wrong, the advantage is zero and you get no gradient. If you have several reward heads and you add them first, you can also lose the difference between “correct but messy” and “correct and clean.” A lot of the variants are just different answers to: which tokens, which sequences, and which groups should actually move the model.

Some of the ones I paid attention to:

- **[GSPO](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2507.18071)** moves importance sampling from the token to the sequence. GRPO clips and weights each token separately. GSPO says the reward is for the whole answer, so the importance ratio should be the sequence likelihood, length-normalized, and you clip the whole response. That is more stable, especially for MoE models where the experts that fire can change after one update and make token-level ratios meaningless.

- **[GDPO](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2601.05242)** is about multiple rewards. Regular GRPO adds the reward metrics and then normalizes once. Different combinations collapse onto the same advantage. GDPO normalizes each reward inside the group first, adds those advantages, then does a batch-level normalize so the scale does not explode as you add more heads.

- **[Dr. GRPO](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)** argues that two extra terms in GRPO are biased. Dividing by response length pushes the model toward short correct answers and long incorrect ones. Dividing by the group standard deviation overweight questions that are too easy or too hard. Removing both terms keeps reasoning accuracy and stops the model from growing wrong answers just to look like it is “thinking longer.”

- **[Beyond the 80/20 Rule](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2506.01939)** looks at token entropy inside a chain of thought. Most tokens are low entropy: they finish a word or keep the current sentence going. A small set of high-entropy tokens are forks: *wait*, *however*, *suppose*. If you only put policy gradients on the top 20% highest-entropy tokens, you get similar or better results than updating every token. Updating only the low-entropy 80% is worse. So a lot of the GRPO update is spent on tokens that were never deciding the path.

- **[Knapsack-RL](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2509.25849)** treats the rollout budget as a knapsack. Easy prompts that the group already solves, and hard prompts that nobody solves, both give zero GRPO gradient. Information gain inside the group is the value of an item. Later epochs put more rollouts on the prompts that still have mixed success, and can go as high as ~90 rollouts on the stubborn ones.

I am not implementing all of these. They are the background for why I picked MGPO. All of them are, in one way or another, trying to spend the update on the part of the batch that still has signal.

# Why MGPO

MGPO comes from [Tiny Model, Big Logic](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2511.06221), the VibeThinker-1.5B report. The paper is not only an RL trick. It is a two-stage story they call the Spectrum-to-Signal Principle.

The usual pipeline is: SFT until Pass@1 looks good, then RL to push Pass@1 a bit more. They say that is the wrong interface. SFT should build a *spectrum*: many different solutions for the same problem, measured by Pass@K, not a single clean answer. RL should then find the *signal* inside that spectrum and raise the probability of the traces that actually work.

How they build the spectrum is a bit involved. They split math into subdomains, probe intermediate SFT checkpoints with Pass@K, keep the most diverse specialist per subdomain, and merge those specialists. I did not redo that SFT recipe. I only took the RL half: MaxEnt-Guided Policy Optimization.

The RL claim is information-theoretic. For one prompt you already have a group of rollouts. Count how many are correct. That gives an empirical success rate \(p_c\). For a binary outcome, entropy is highest at \(p_c = 0.5\). At 50% the model is on the edge: it is not always right and it is not always wrong. That is the group with the most information gain.

The paper does not use entropy itself as the weight. It measures how far \(p_c\) is from 0.5 with a KL they call Max-Entropy Deviation Distance, then turns that into an exponential weight:

\[
w_{\mathrm{ME}}(p_c) = \exp(-\lambda \cdot D_{\mathrm{ME}}(p_c \,\|\, 0.5))
\]

\(\lambda = 0\) is regular GRPO. Larger \(\lambda\) makes a sharper bell around 50%. That weight multiplies the GRPO advantage for every rollout in the group. Groups near 80% all-correct get down-weighted because the model already knows this prompt, and you do not want it to overfit the few traces that happened to work. Groups near 20% get down-weighted because at high rollout counts that can just be luck. The sweet spot is the struggle.

That is the part I wanted to test. MulSeT groups are noisy in exactly this way. The same scene can produce a clean geometric argument or a completely derailed one, and a 50/50 split is common.

# Why MulSeT

[MulSeT](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2509.02359) (Multi-view Spatial Understanding Tasks) was built to study why multimodal models are still bad at space. The authors look at single-view, multi-view, and video. Single-view is close to human level. Multi-view and video are not. They also argue that just adding more training data does not fix the hard cases, and that positional encoding in the vision encoder matters more than positional encoding in the language model.

The multi-view split is the one I use. Each sample is two images of the same simulated indoor scene, taken from different angles. Some objects appear in both views, some only in one. There are three task types, all four-option multiple choice:

1. **Occlusion restoration.** An object is masked in the second image. Identify it using the first view.
2. **Distance comparison.** Which object is closest to a shared reference object in both images.
3. **Azimuth transfer.** Stand in the first camera, face a reference object, and say where another object sits in the second view: left front, left rear, right front, right rear.

Humans get about 86% on distance, 58% on occlusion, and 51% on azimuth. So the last one is not a toy. The training set is 30k pairs, the benchmark is 8.2k, from more than 5,000 AI2-THOR scenes.

The interesting property for MGPO is that one question really does have several valid reasoning traces, and they do not look like each other. You can lock the two views together through the carpet, or through a table shadow, or through a shared plant. If one of those correspondences is wrong, the rest of the chain is wrong. That is closer to “many solutions for the same problem” than a math contest problem where the traces often share the same skeleton.

A typical azimuth question looks like this:

> Where is the beanbag in the second image located from your perspective?
> Choose the correct option from the list below. Only respond with the corresponding letter.
>
> A. left front
> B. left rear
> C. right front
> D. right rear

One trace might say: the carpet in the top right of image two is the bottom center of image one, so the beanbag is right-front. Another trace might use the table shadow. Both can be valid. Both can also be one object-match away from a completely different letter. Simple misdirection is enough to derail the whole process. That is why this set is a better stress test for group-level weighting than a dataset where most rollouts fail for the same reason.

# Experiment

I ran GRPO and MGPO on the same MulSeT setup with Gemma 3 4B. I did not use a base Qwen3-VL for this comparison. Qwen3-VL needed a higher max length and spent it on verbose traces that did not help. Gemma 3 VL is also not branded as a Thinking or Instruction checkpoint in the same way, so the starting behavior is different. I am treating this as a small controlled run on one model, not a bake-off across families.

The training signal is the usual group setup: several rollouts per prompt, verifiable multiple-choice reward, MGPO reweighting the group when the success rate is away from 50%. I am not claiming I reproduced the full VibeThinker SFT spectrum. This is only the RL weighting on top of GRPO.

# Results

GRPO is pretty much flat on reward. It sits around 1.25–1.30 for most of the run. MGPO starts a bit lower and then keeps rising, finishing closer to 1.40. That matches the paper’s bet: if you stop spending as much update on groups that are already solved or still random, the remaining groups actually move the policy.

KL is the more useful diagnostic. GRPO stays almost on zero for the whole 225 steps. The policy is barely leaving the reference. MGPO’s smoothed KL walks up in steps and sits around 0.2 by the end. The raw MGPO KL does spike (some batches hit ~1–1.7), but the trend is a controlled climb, not a collapse. Higher than GRPO, relatively stable. That is the point: the model is allowed to move, and the move is not random walk.

Completion length tells the same story from the other side. GRPO drifts longer and is noisier, settling near 440 with spikes toward 480. MGPO stays near 420 with a tight band. After training, mean response length is 472 (base), 493 (GRPO), 464 (MGPO): 8 tokens under base and 29 under GRPO. So MGPO is not buying reward by writing more.

I read that as MGPO doing what it says on the tin. Groups near 50% get a larger advantage scale, so the policy takes a bigger step on the prompts where the traces disagree. Spatial MulSeT is full of those prompts. GRPO treats a 50/50 group and an 80/20 group more uniformly after the usual mean/std normalize, so the hard scenes do not get extra weight and both reward and KL stay flat.

## Benchmarks

Gemma 3 4B, same eval. MGPO deltas in the first table are versus GRPO. Length deltas are versus base, then versus GRPO.

| Benchmark | Base | GRPO | MGPO |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| RealWorldQA | 44.2 | 44.2 | 45.2 (+1.0) |
| MMStar | 40.9 | 39.4 | 40.4 (+1.0) |
| MulSeT | 28.4 | 32.4 | 33.6 (+1.2) |
| BLINK | 44.7 | 42.7 | 41.1 (−1.6) |

| | Base | GRPO | MGPO |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Response length | 472 | 493 | 464 (−8, −29) |

MulSeT is the training domain, so the +5.2 from base to GRPO and the extra +1.2 from MGPO are the numbers I care about. RealWorldQA ticks up a point. MMStar recovers the point GRPO dropped. BLINK is the miss: both RL runs go down, MGPO more so. I do not want to oversell a 4B run. What I wanted to check was whether MGPO changes training dynamics on a visual task with many disagreeing traces. On this run, yes. Reward moves. KL moves in a higher but stable band. Completions stay short. GRPO does none of that in a useful way.
